A job queue can work perfectly while every worker is healthy.

But workers crash. Processes restart. Machines lose connectivity. A handler can take longer than expected or get stuck.

If a worker claims a job and disappears, the queue needs a way to recover the work without leaving it active forever.

That problem led me to implement lease-based ownership and recovery in JobRail.

## Why marking a job active isn't enough

Suppose a worker claims a job and the queue marks it as active.

The worker then crashes before completing the job.

If the queue only knows that the job is active, it may have no reason to believe the worker has disappeared. The job can remain stuck in that state.

Simply moving every active job back to the waiting queue is also dangerous. A worker might still be processing the job, even if it temporarily cannot communicate with Redis.

The queue needs a better way to determine whether ownership is still valid.

## The lease concept

A lease represents temporary ownership of a job.

Instead of treating a claim as permanent, the system records ownership that must remain valid over time.

JobRail uses lease-related ownership and recovery mechanisms. Workers can renew their leases through heartbeats, and the system can recover jobs whose ownership has expired.

Conceptually, the lifecycle looks like this:

1. A worker claims a job.
2. The queue records the worker's ownership.
3. The worker processes the job and renews its lease as needed.
4. If the worker completes successfully, the job transitions to completion.
5. If the worker disappears and its lease expires, recovery can make the job eligible for processing again.

This gives the queue a way to detect abandoned work without assuming every active job is still being processed.

## Why ownership matters

Expiration by itself doesn't solve every race condition.

Imagine that worker A's lease expires, and the queue recovers the job. Worker B then claims it.

What if worker A wakes up and tries to complete the old job?

The system needs to validate that worker A still has the correct ownership before accepting the state transition.

This is where ownership tokens and guarded state changes become important. A stale worker must not be allowed to overwrite the newer owner's state simply because it once held the job.

Even then, leases don't magically prevent every duplicate side effect. If worker A already sent an email or charged an external payment before losing its lease, the queue cannot undo that action.

For side-effecting operations, idempotency and appropriate external-system safeguards are still necessary.

## What this taught me

I initially thought of a worker as something that takes a job and executes it.

Thinking about leases changed that model. A worker is participating in a temporary ownership protocol, and the system has to handle the possibility that ownership expires while the worker is still running.

That makes recovery, heartbeats, ownership validation, and state transitions closely related concerns.

It also highlights an important distributed-systems lesson: a timeout can indicate that something has gone wrong, but it doesn't always prove that the original process has stopped.

This is one of the areas I want to keep improving because failure handling is what separates a queue that works in a demo from one that can be trusted with real workloads.
