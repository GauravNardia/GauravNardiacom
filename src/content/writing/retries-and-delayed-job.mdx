A background job will fail eventually.

An email provider might time out. An external API might return an error. A network connection might disappear at the wrong moment.

Immediately treating every failure as permanent would make a queue unnecessarily fragile. But retrying everything immediately can be just as problematic.

I wanted JobRail to distinguish a failed attempt from a job that has permanently failed.

## Why retry immediately?

Suppose an external service is temporarily unavailable and hundreds of jobs depend on it.

If every job retries immediately, they all hit the same service again. If the service is still struggling, the retries add even more pressure.

This can turn a temporary problem into a larger outage.

I wanted a way to delay retries and spread them out over time.

## Exponential backoff

JobRail supports exponential backoff for retries.

The basic idea is to increase the waiting period after each failed attempt.

A simplified formula is:

delay = min(max_delay, base_delay × 2^(attempt - 1))

For example, with a base delay of one second and a maximum delay of thirty seconds, the delays would be approximately:

- Attempt 1: 1 second
- Attempt 2: 2 seconds
- Attempt 3: 4 seconds
- Attempt 4: 8 seconds
- Attempt 5: 16 seconds

Later attempts: capped at 30 seconds

The values here are an illustrative example, not a claim about JobRail's configured production defaults.

A maximum-attempt limit is also important. Without one, a permanently failing job could continue retrying indefinitely.

## Separating delayed work from waiting work

A job that is eligible to run now is different from a job that should run in the future.

JobRail uses Redis-backed queue structures to distinguish waiting jobs from delayed or scheduled work.

The scheduler can identify jobs whose delay has expired and make them eligible for processing.

This separation means workers don't have to treat every future job as immediately executable.

It also creates a new correctness requirement: promoting a delayed job must not accidentally make the same job eligible multiple times.

That is why scheduling and state transitions need careful coordination.

## The failure cases I had to consider

A retry system raises more questions than it first appears to.

- What if the worker crashes before scheduling the retry?
- What if the scheduler processes the same due job more than once?
- What if a retry becomes eligible while workers are busy?
- What happens when the maximum number of attempts is reached?
- How do we distinguish an individual failed attempt from the final job state?

These questions affect the data model, scheduling logic, and recovery behavior.

The system needs to maintain a coherent relationship between the job's current state, its attempt history, and its next eligible execution time.

## What I learned

Retries aren't just a loop around a function call. They're part of the queue's state machine.

A good retry design needs to consider timing, failure limits, duplicate execution, and recovery.

Exponential backoff helps reduce repeated pressure on a struggling dependency, but it doesn't solve every reliability problem. Some workloads may also benefit from jitter, which randomizes retry delays to avoid many jobs retrying at exactly the same time.

Building this feature made me think about retries as a system-level behavior rather than a small implementation detail.
